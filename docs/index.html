01 Why it works now

Sounds and words land in the same space

EmbeddingGemma 2 embeds audio and text into one 768-d space. A clip and a text query are compared directly by cosine similarity, with no labels, classifiers or training. Search is nearest-neighbour lookup.

What the 48 ratings tell us
  1. How people talk is easy: 27 of 32. Banter, bedtime voices, news anchors, kids' character voices, storytelling. The embedding captures speaking style and mood very well.
  2. Specific sounds are hard: 5 of 12. Whispering, music, rain. These need fine distinctions, or sounds that podcasts rarely contain.
  3. Interaction is hardest: 1 of 4. It found the mechanics (people talking over each other) but not the emotion (hostility). The shows here rarely argue for real.
  4. Misses are near-misses. “Whispering” returned soft bedtime voices; “rain” returned calm ASMR. Right mood, wrong detail.
  5. The top 4 are about equally good (#1 right 8 of 12 times, #4 9). It finds the right neighbourhood, but not the single best clip.
03 A finding about the space

Closing the gap between words and sounds finds the right shows, but loses the vibe

In the raw space, text sits closer to other text and audio to other audio than either does to the other: the modality gap. Subtracting each side's average vector (centring) moves words in among the clips they describe. A second listening round then showed what that costs.

Centring pulled results toward what shows are about: “upbeat music and singing” returned shows that talk about music, and “excited sports talk” returned calm stretches of sports shows.

Single words find the right kind of show far more often

Of each word's 25 nearest clips, how many come from the show type it describes. That's a category test, not a vibe test.
The gap, in numbers

Mean cosine: . Search still works without the fix: it only needs a word's nearest clips to be right, not for the word to sit among them.

The fix is two fixed vectors: the audio mean over the catalogue and the text mean over neutral phrases. At query time it's one subtraction, so it would be cheap at Spotify scale.

The study: how much of the gap should search close?

Search was run at 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of the correction. A listener rated each version's top 4 clips ( ratings in all, blind to the version), and the automatic category check ran alongside.

Share of top results that sounded right, by how much of the gap is closed (axis starts at 40%)
Short searches, clip by clip (top 4 · green = sounded right)
What we learned
  1. It's a cliff, not a slope. Long searches hold at from 0% to 75% of the correction, then drop to at 100%.
  2. Centring never helps by ear. Short searches go as more of the gap is closed.
  3. It strips the sound and keeps the topic. Searches defined by a sound lose (“music” , “whispering” ), while topic searches (“sports”, “news”, “crime”, “football”) stay at 4 of 4 throughout.
  4. The automatic check can't see any of this. It stays flat at , and the per-word table above counted music talk shows as hits for “music”. Only listening showed the loss.
  5. Raw embeddings already work for short searches: one- or two-word searches sounded right. Decision: search stays uncentred. Centring is a switch (rank.center) for topic lookups, not for vibe.
04 What we learned about the model

Beyond the search: what this taught us about audio–text embeddings

Every claim is labelled by how sure we are: Measured Tried & failed Hypothesis Lesson

What the model is good and bad at
Measured

Speaking style is its strength

Searches about how people talk scored 27 of 32; specific sounds 5 of 12; interaction 1 of 4.

The ratings ↑
Measured

It hears the mechanics, not the emotion

For “heated argument” it found people talking over each other, but none of them were hostile. Overlapping voices are a sound; hostility is a meaning.

Measured

Two models, opposite strengths

In the first test, CLAP (trained on sound events) won on laughter and whispering (5 of 5 vs 3 of 5). EmbeddingGemma 2 won on speech styles: news anchor 5 vs 2, sports commentary 4 vs 0, crime narration 4 vs 1. (top 5 from a fitting show type)

Hypothesis

It also hears the show itself

“More like this” stays in the same show 58% of the time (2% by chance), and the map clusters by show. Same hosts, mic and music give each show a sound fingerprint, so part of “vibe” is really production.

Measured

The top results are a neighbourhood, not a ranking

Result #1 sounded right 8 of 12 times, #4 9. The model finds the right area; order within the top few is noise.

The modality gap, and our attempt to close it
Measured

Text and audio live apart in the shared space

Mean cosine . Projected together, 0 of 10 of a word's nearest neighbours are clips.

The plots ↑
Measured

The gap doesn't break search

Ranking only needs the order of similarity, and every clip pays roughly the same “crossing cost”. Hence 33 of 48 sounded right with the gap.

Tried & failed

Closing the gap made search worse

Subtracting each side's mean (centring) closed the gap on the map (0 → 7 of 10 neighbours are clips), but by ear long searches fell 33 → 27 and short ones 31 → 26. Partial centring (25–75%) gained nothing (32–33). Goal not met.

Hypothesis

Why: the “average” carries part of the sound

Subtracting the mean seems to remove part of what makes clips sound different, leaving topic. Evidence: “music” started returning shows that talk about music (2 → 0), while topic words stayed 4 of 4. Untested next step: remove only the main gap direction.

Lessons for building with embeddings
Lesson

The cheap metric pointed the wrong way

The automatic category check stayed flat or rose with centring while listeners heard it get worse. It even scored “music” 11 → 19 by counting music talk shows. Only listening caught it.

Measured

Zero-shot depends on what you contrast with

Laughter as “people laughing” minus “people talking” rated ASMR above comedy: anything non-speech looked like laughter. Contrasting speech with speech fixed it.

Measured

Long LLM prose embeds poorly against audio

The LLM's flowery 30-word sound descriptions pulled in odd matches (“thoughtful slow interview” → ASMR). Embedding the user's own words with the description, together with the empty-transcript fix, lifted the category check from 54% to 67% on the first run (the two fixes weren't measured separately).

Measured

Empty inputs collapse to one point

87 near-silent clips had empty transcripts, so all got the same text embedding, which happened to sit near “sports”. Slow Radio topped “excited sports talk” until we stopped scoring topic on clips with fewer than 15 words.

Lesson

Precision matters on a laptop

The model defaults to bfloat16, which ran 12× slower on this CPU. float32 brought it to 3 s per clip (float16 gives NaNs).

06 How it was built

Four steps, all on a laptop

1
Cut39 public podcasts, 3 episodes each, cut into 2,808 clips of 20 seconds.
2
EmbedEach clip → a 768-d audio embedding (EmbeddingGemma 2, open weights, fp32 on CPU, ~3 s per clip). Beat LAION-CLAP 70% vs 51% in a 15-prompt test.
3
SearchAn LLM rewrites the query as a sound description; it's embedded and ranked by cosine. Score = 0.4 embedding + 0.4 measured features (pace, pauses, energy, zero-shot laughter) + 0.2 transcript topic.
4
ListenA person rated the top results, without seeing the show names.

07 Caveats

Read with care

  • Small test: one listener (the builder), 48 clips, and no random clips mixed in, so there's no “chance” score to compare with. Next: a few listeners, with random clips mixed in blind.
  • Data gaps decide the misses: few shows have crowds, music, real arguments or nature sounds, so those searches are weak. That's why “a crowd cheering” and “music playing” aren't map buttons: their nearest clips are mostly ASMR.
  • Small corpus: 39 English-language shows, minutes 2–10 of each episode.
  • Playback: audio streams from each publisher; about half are blocked on the office network, where vibe serve plays a local copy.
  • Internal prototype using third-party shows. Full numbers, including an earlier automatic check: reports/REPORT.md.
