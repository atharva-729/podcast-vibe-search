01 Embeddings

An embedding is a list of numbers that says where a thing lives on a map of meaning

Real life

Describe a song with sliders: energy 0.9, sadness 0.1, tempo 0.8, acoustic 0.2. Now every song is a point in a 4-slider space, and songs with similar slider values sound similar. You could find “songs like this one” by finding the nearest points. That's the whole trick.

An embedding model does this automatically, but with 768 sliders instead of 4, and nobody chose what the sliders mean. The model learned them from huge amounts of data, so that things with similar meaning get similar numbers. A 768-slider list is called a 768-dimensional vector, or just “768-d”.

A pretend 2-slider version. Similar things cluster: laughing near giggling, rain near waves. Real embeddings do exactly this, just in 768 dimensions you can't draw.
In our project

Every 20-second podcast clip goes into EmbeddingGemma 2 and comes out as 768 numbers. We have 2,808 clips, so the whole catalogue is a table of 2,808 rows × 768 columns (index/audio_emb.npy). Searching is “find the rows nearest to the query's row”.

TL;DR

Embedding = coordinates on a meaning map. Near = similar. 768-d = 768 coordinates.

02 Cosine similarity

“Near” is measured by the angle between arrows, not the distance

Picture every embedding as an arrow from the centre of the map. Two things are similar when their arrows point the same way. Cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle between them:

  • same direction (0°) → cosine 1.0: identical meaning
  • at right angles (90°) → cosine 0: unrelated
  • opposite (180°) → cosine −1: opposite meaning

We also make every arrow length 1 first (L2-normalising), so only the direction counts. A loud clip and a quiet clip of the same vibe shouldn't differ just because one arrow is longer.

Drag on the canvas to point the query arrow.
Each grey arrow is a “clip”. The one with the highest cosine to your query wins, and that's literally how our search ranks clips (in 768-d instead of 2-d).
In our project

Search embeds your text, takes the cosine with all 2,808 clip vectors (one matrix multiply, milliseconds), and keeps the highest. That's nearest-neighbour search. No database tricks needed at this size.

03 Multimodal

“Multimodal” means sound and text get coordinates on the same map

A modality is just a type of input: text, audio, images, video. Older models had one map per modality: a text map and a separate sound map. The coordinates meant different things, so you couldn't compare a sentence with a clip.

Real life

An English speaker and a Japanese speaker each describe the same sunset. Translate both into one shared language and you can compare the descriptions directly. A multimodal model is that translator: it maps a laughing sound and the words “people laughing” to nearby coordinates.

How does it learn that? Contrastive training, roughly

The model is shown millions of (audio, caption) pairs, like a clip of rain with the caption “rain on a window”. Training pulls each true pair's arrows together and pushes mismatched pairs apart. Do that enough times and “rain”-sounding audio ends up pointing the same way as the word “rain”.

🔊 20 s clip→EmbeddingGemma 2→768-d· cosine ·768-d←EmbeddingGemma 2←“people laughing”
In our project

This is the idea the project tests: can a text vibe find matching sound with no labels at all? We tried two multimodal models on 15 prompts × 300 random clips. EmbeddingGemma 2 put a fitting show in the top 5 70% of the time, against CLAP (an older audio-text model) at 51% and random picking at 13%. It costs about 8× more compute (3.1 s vs 0.37 s per clip on a laptop CPU), and it was worth it.

Gotcha we hit

EmbeddingGemma 2 loads in bfloat16 by default, which is 12× slower on a laptop CPU without native support. We force float32. (And never float16: it produces NaNs.)

04 What we built

From podcast feeds to ranked moments, step by step

  1. Feeds → clips. 39 public podcasts (picked for a spread of vibes), 3 recent episodes each, minutes 2–10 (skipping intros and ads), cut into 20-second clips: 2,808 in total.
  2. Audio embedding of every clip (section 03): index/audio_emb.npy.
  3. Transcript of every clip with Whisper (an open speech-to-text model, run locally). It gives the words plus when each word was said.
  4. Measured features (plain code, no AI):
    • energy: loudness (RMS volume in decibels, via librosa) and how much it varies
    • pauses: share of the clip that is more than 25 dB quieter than the episode's loud parts
    • expressiveness: how much the voice pitch moves (librosa's yin pitch tracker)
    • pace: words per minute, from Whisper's word timings
    • laughter / music / crowd: zero-shot with the embedding (see the box below)
  5. Percentiles. Every feature becomes a 0–1 rank within the corpus: 0.9 energy means louder than 90% of our clips. That puts “pace” and “decibels” on the same scale.
Zero-shot, explained

We have no laughter detector. So we ask the embedding: is this clip's arrow closer to “a group of friends laughing out loud at a joke” or to “a person speaking seriously”? The difference is the laughter score. “Zero-shot” = no training, just comparing with text.

The first version compared “people laughing” with “people talking”, and ASMR scored as laughter because it wasn't talking! Contrasting speech with speech fixed it: comedy now scores highest on laughter.

At search time

Your query goes to a cheap LLM (gpt-5-mini, about $0.0003, cached), which rewrites it as (a) a description of the sound, (b) feature targets like energy: high, laughter: high, and (c) a topic like football. Then every clip gets a score:

score = 0.4 × sound match (cosine: query ↔ clip audio) + 0.4 × feature fit (how close the clip's percentiles are to the targets) + 0.2 × topic match (cosine: topic ↔ clip transcript)

Each part is rescaled to 0–1 per query (min-max), so they add fairly. Max 2 clips per show, and adjacent clips are merged. The “why it matched” chips (“high energy · lots of laughter”) are written from these numbers, not by an LLM.

Two fixes we learned the hard way

1. Clips with no speech (rain, ambient) have empty transcripts, and all empty transcripts got the same embedding, which happened to match “sports”. Slow Radio came top for “excited sports talk”. Now clips with fewer than 15 words get no topic score.

2. The LLM's sound descriptions are long and flowery, and long text embeds poorly against audio. So the sound match uses your words + the LLM's description together.

05 UMAP

You can't draw 768 dimensions, so UMAP squashes them to 2 while keeping neighbours together

Real life

A world map squashes a 3-D globe onto flat paper. Something gets distorted (Greenland looks huge), but neighbouring countries stay neighbours. UMAP does that for 768-d → 2-d: it tries hard to keep each point's nearest neighbours nearby on the flat picture.

Our real map: all 2,808 clips, coloured by show type. Clusters = clips that sound alike.

How to read it:

  • Close dots really are similar in 768-d. That's the part UMAP protects.
  • Distances between far-apart clusters mean little. Two islands on opposite sides aren't necessarily “most different”.
  • Axes have no meaning. There's no “x = energy”. Don't read them.
  • Clips cluster by show a lot, because a show keeps its sound (same hosts, same mic, same music). That's also why “more like this” stays in the same show 58% of the time.
06 Testing

We had two ways to test, and one of them lied

Test 1: the automatic category check

Search “sports talk” and count how many of the top 10 come from shows labelled Sports in iTunes. Free and instant.

Result: 71% vs 13% for random picks.

Test 2: the listening test

A person listens to the top 4 clips for 12 searches, without seeing the show name, and answers one question: does it sound like the search?

Result: 33 of 48 sounded right.

Why test 1 is a proxy

Judging a restaurant by the cuisine on its sign vs actually tasting the food. “Italian” on the sign doesn't mean this dish is good pasta. Likewise, a category describes the show, not the 20 seconds: a quiet, serious stretch of a comedy show still counts as “Comedy”, and the label can't tell you whether anyone is laughing.

What the listening test said, grouped by kind of search

  • How people talk (banter, bedtime voice, news anchor) is easy: the embedding captures speaking style and mood.
  • Specific sounds (music, rain, whispering) are hard: they need fine detail, or the podcasts just don't contain them.
  • Interaction is hardest: “heated argument” found people talking over each other, but not the hostility. It got the mechanics, not the emotion.
  • Misses are usually near-misses: “whispering” returned soft bedtime voices, which have the right mood but the wrong detail.
TL;DR

Proxy metrics are cheap but measure something adjacent. When they disagree with a human listening, believe the human. Section 08 shows the metric pointing exactly the wrong way.

07 The modality gap

Text and audio share one map, but they live in different neighbourhoods of it

“Same map” doesn't mean “mixed together”. We measured the average cosine between random pairs:

Text is more similar to other text, and audio to other audio, than either is to the other. Every text arrow carries a shared “I am text” lean, and every audio arrow a shared “I am audio” lean. That systematic offset between the two clouds is called the modality gap. It's well known; CLIP (images + text) has it too.

Real life

Two groups of people standing in a field: the audio people near the left fence, the text people near the right fence. Within each group, people stand near whoever they're similar to: the rain fan next to the rain fan. But everyone on the text side is far from everyone on the audio side, just because of which fence they're at.

Here's the key: if you ask “which audio person is closest to text-person ‘rain’?”, you can still get the right answer. Everyone pays the same walking tax to cross the field, so the ranking survives. That's why our search worked before we knew about the gap.

Close the gap:
Circles = clips (audio), squares = words (text). Lines join each word to its nearest clip, green if it's the right partner. Slide to subtract the offset: watch the clouds overlap. Notice the lines are mostly right even at 0%. That's the “same walking tax”.
In our project

With all clips and words projected together (section 09 has the real plot), 0 of 10 of a word's nearest neighbours are clips. The words sit on their own island.

08 Centring

Centring: subtract each side's average, and the clouds line up

Real life

A basketball team and a jockey club each measure “tall”. Raw heights say every basketball player is taller than every jockey. Pointless. Measure each person relative to their own group's average (subtract the group mean) and now “tall for a jockey” and “tall for a player” are comparable. That's centring.

clip' = normalise( clip − strength × mean(all clips) ) query' = normalise( query − strength × mean(77 neutral phrases) )
  • The audio mean is computed once over the whole catalogue.
  • The text mean is computed once over 77 neutral phrases (“a man talking”, “rain”, “applause”…). They're deliberately not our test searches, so we couldn't accidentally tune to them.
  • At query time it's one subtraction. Spotify could do this at full scale with no retraining. strength is how much of the gap to close: 0 = raw, 1 = full centring.

What it fixed

Single words found the right kind of show far more often (of each word's 25 nearest clips):

The twist: by ear, it got worse

Your listening test: 33 → 27 of 48 after full centring. Then a study at 0 / 25 / 50 / 75 / 100% of the correction:

Listener score (solid lines) vs the automatic category check (dashed), against how much of the gap is closed. The axis starts at 40%.
  • It's a cliff, not a slope: long searches are flat until 75%, then fall at 100%.
  • Short searches slide steadily down.
  • The automatic check stays flat: completely blind to what you heard.

Why? Centring strips the sound and keeps the topic

Look at which searches lost: “music” went (it started returning shows that talk about music) and “whispering” (soft, normal voices). Topic words like “sports”, “news”, “crime” and “football” stayed at 4 of 4 throughout.

Our best explanation (a hypothesis, honestly labelled)

The average audio vector isn't just an “I am audio” tag. Part of what makes clips sound different, like music vs speech or whisper vs voice, seems to live along that shared direction. Subtract it and re-normalise, and what's left is weighted more toward meaning/topic. So matching gets more “on-topic” and less “on-sound”.

Like adjusting a photo to remove a yellow tint: great when the tint was the lamp, bad when the subject was a sunflower.

TL;DR

Centring closes the gap (words land among their clips) and finds the right shows, but loses the vibe. A category metric said ship it; listening said don't. Search stays uncentred (rank.center: false).

10 Quiz

Check yourself (click to reveal)

1. Why can a text search find audio at all?

Because a multimodal model puts both into one shared 768-d space, trained so that matching (sound, caption) pairs point the same way. Cosine between them measures match.

2. If text and audio live in separate neighbourhoods (the gap), why did search still work?

Ranking only needs the order of closeness. Every clip pays roughly the same “crossing tax”, so the nearest clip to a word is still the right one, as in the slider toy at 0%.

3. What exactly does centring subtract?

The average audio vector (over all clips) from every clip, and the average text vector (over 77 neutral phrases) from every query, then it re-normalises. Two fixed vectors, one subtraction each.

4. Centring improved the automatic check. Why didn't we turn it on?

The listening test went 33 → 27 of 48 (long) and 31 → 26 of 40 (short). The automatic check measures show category (≈ topic), and centring improves topic while hurting sound. The metric was blind to the loss.

5. Why did “music” fail after centring?

It returned shows that talk about music (Dissect, Song Exploder): right topic, no actual music. Centring shifted matching from sound toward meaning.

6. What do the axes of the UMAP map mean?

Nothing. Only local neighbourhoods are meaningful; far-apart distances and directions aren't.

7. Why is “laughter” a zero-shot score and not a detector?

We never trained on laughter. We compare each clip's embedding with two text descriptions (laughing friends vs serious speaker) and use the difference.

Glossary

embedding / vectorA list of numbers (here 768) giving something's position on a meaning map.
768-d768 dimensions = 768 numbers per item.
cosine similarityHow aligned two arrows are: 1 same direction, 0 unrelated.
L2-normaliseRescale an arrow to length 1 so only direction counts.
modalityA type of input: text, audio, image, video.
multimodal modelOne model that embeds several modalities into one space.
contrastive trainingPull true pairs together, push mismatched pairs apart.
modality gapText and audio vectors sit in offset regions of the shared space.
centringSubtract each modality's mean vector (by a strength 0–1) and re-normalise.
nearest-neighbour searchReturn the items with the highest cosine to the query.
UMAPSquashes high-d data to 2-d while keeping each point's neighbours close.
zero-shotClassifying by comparing with text descriptions, no training.
percentileRank within our corpus, 0–1. 0.9 = more than 90% of clips.
min-maxRescale scores so the lowest is 0 and highest 1, per query.
proxy metricSomething cheap that's correlated with what you care about, but isn't it.
WhisperOpen speech-to-text model; gives words + timestamps.
